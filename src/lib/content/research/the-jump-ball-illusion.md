> **The short version**
>
> - **The prize is worthless.** The rules hand the tip loser the next two quarter-openers. Over a game, the tip winner ends up level on possessions.
> - **Tips are a personal skill.** Andre Drummond wins 73% of his jumps. Domantas Sabonis wins 29%.
> - **The tip reads quality.** Compare equal teams and three of the five points vanish. Add the betting line and about one is left.
> - **One tip matters.** The overtime jump ball is worth a real half-possession, and nobody gives it back.

## Two seconds, five points

Every NBA game opens with the same small ceremony. Two very tall men crouch at center court, a referee lofts the ball, and one team comes down with it. Half the arena is still finding its seats. Nobody builds a game plan around it. Yet across 29 seasons of possession-by-possession data, 1997-98 through 2025-26, the team that wins the opening tip goes on to win **52.8%** of its games.

Put it the way a fan would feel it. When the home team controls the tip it wins **61.4%** of its games. When it loses the tip that falls to **56.3%**. That five-point swing hangs on a two-second toss between big men.

If the tip caused that swing, it would be one of the great free lunches in sports. It doesn't. The jump ball is better understood as a small measuring device. It does not decide the game. It *knows things about* the game. This is the story of what it knows, and of the one jump ball all night that is worth winning.

## The thing being won is nearly worthless

Start with what the tip actually buys, because the answer is almost nothing.

The NBA's rules neutralize the jump ball. The team that loses the tip gets the first possession of the second *and* third quarters. The tip winner opens the first and fourth. (In these 36,765 games that alternation held 99.9% of the time.) Add it up and the tip winner finishes a game with, on average, **no extra possessions**: the gap is under one hundredth of a possession, smaller than the noise. In 37% of games the counts come out exactly even, and in virtually every game they are within two.

The accounting shows up quarter by quarter, like a fingerprint. The tip winner gets about half an extra possession in the first quarter and half an extra in the fourth, and gives back half in each of the second and third. The scoreboard follows. Tip winners outscore their opponents in the first and fourth quarters and get outscored in the second and third. That is the rulebook at work, with no momentum or effort behind it.

<!-- interactive:quarters -->

The prize itself is poor. The opening possession of the game scores **0.97 points** on average, against **1.08** for all possessions. Every quarter-opening possession is below average (about 0.99), probably because the defense is set and there is no transition to run, and the game's first possession is the weakest of the four. The grand prize at center court is a below-average possession, handed over on loan and repaid by halftime.

So the tip is worth roughly nothing. Then why do tip winners win 52.8% of the time?

## Winning tips belongs to one person

The first clue is that winning tips is not a coin flip.

If jump balls were pure luck, almost every team's season-long tip rate would land between 39% and 61%. The actual range runs from **17% to 84%**. The 2002-03 and 2003-04 Cavaliers, jumping 7'3" Žydrūnas Ilgauskas, won 84% of their tips two years running. The 2009-10 and 2010-11 Rockets, with Yao Ming hurt, mostly sent 6'9" power forward Luis Scola out to jump and won 17% two years running. Split any season into alternating games and the two halves' tip rates track each other (a correlation of 0.64, against 0.80 for win rates). The 2001-02 Heat, with Alonzo Mourning jumping nearly every night, once won **26 tips in a row**. Shaq's 2000-01 Lakers ran off 23 straight.

The skill belongs to one man, not the roster. About seven in eight opening jumps are taken by a center or a center/forward, and the average jumper stands a shade over 6'11". Reach is the raw material.

<!-- interactive:height -->

Height is only part of it. Timing and bounce matter, and individual jumpers are consistent from year to year (a correlation of 0.74 between one season and the next). Shawn Bradley and Yao Ming both stood 7'6". Bradley won nearly two-thirds of his jumps and Yao won 41%. Ben Wallace, at 6'9", won 61%. Nikola Jokić, maybe the best center of his generation, is a coin flip at 51.5%.

Hold that thought, because it is the key to everything. *Being good at tips and being good at basketball are different skills that happen to live in similar bodies.*

## The tip is a measurement, not a weapon

Better teams do win more tips. Across 863 team-seasons the relationship between tip rate and win rate is clear but loose, because good teams tend to employ good centers, and good centers tend to be enormous and springy. An extra 10 points of tip rate travels with about 3 extra wins per 82 games. It travels with them. It does not cause them, and the exceptions give the game away.

<!-- interactive:scatter -->

Here is a cleaner way to see through it: a time-machine test. If the tip helps you win tonight, then tonight's tip should predict tonight's result, and a jump ball in some *other* game should not. But it does. Regular-season teams that go on to win the tip in their **next** game, a jump ball that has not happened yet, win tonight 51.3% of the time, against 48.8% for teams about to lose it.

<!-- interactive:placebo -->

Squeeze from the other side. Compare only games between the same two teams, in the same building, in the same season, where the tip went one way in some games and the other way in others, and the five-point gap shrinks to about **2.3 points**. Rate every team's strength using only its earlier results, then compare tip winners to tip losers of equal strength: about **1.9 points** survive. Roughly three of the five points vanish once you account for who is playing whom. The full ladder, with the betting line at the bottom, comes later.

## The tip takes attendance

Is the last couple of points the tip's real power? Mostly no, because the tip measures something game-specific that a season-long rating cannot see: **who actually takes the tip tonight**.

The opening tip is taken by your starting center, who is usually one of your best players. When he does not take it (hurt, resting, traded, or swapped out for the matchup), two things happen at once. Your tip rate collapses and your team gets worse. Take the 573 team-seasons where a team's usual jumper took at least 20 opening tips and did not take at least 10 others. In the games he did not jump, the team won **7.4 points fewer tips** and **4.0 points fewer games**. The tip result is partly a health report: win it, and it is a little more likely your best big man is on the floor.

Feed that one fact, whether the usual jumper was out there, into the strength-adjusted comparison and the leftover edge drops from 1.9 points to about **1.4**. It is a crude flag. It knows nothing about the other four starters, minute limits or back-to-backs, which all travel with center absences. A sharper cut makes the point. In games where both teams' usual jumpers took the tip, the leftover edge is about **1 point**, too small to tell apart from zero. In games where at least one usual jumper was missing, it is about **3 points**. (Both figures adjust for team strength.)

The playoffs lean the same way, though not conclusively. In the postseason, when everyone who can walk plays, tip winners win 51.8%. That is lower, but within the noise for 2,368 games.

One more thing the tip knows is where the game is. Home teams win **51.4%** of opening tips. Personnel is not the reason, because the *same jumper* wins about 3 points more of his jumps at home than on the road. Whatever home court is made of (sleep, familiarity, crowd), a trace shows up in a two-second toss. It is a small piece, worth about a quarter of a point of the headline number.

## Ask the bookmakers

A strength rating only knows results. Bookmakers know more. By tip-off their price folds in injuries, rest, back-to-backs and how a matchup is likely to go. If the leftover edge is hidden team quality, the betting line should soak it up.

So we matched **12,937** regular-season games from 2011-12 through 2021-22 to [archived pregame moneylines](https://github.com/flancast90/sportsbookreview-scraper), stripped out the bookmaker's margin, and ran the same comparison on exactly the same games. Two rows of the ladder are new: the betting line on its own, and the betting line plus the usual-jumper flags. Switch the toggle to see both sets of games.

<!-- interactive:ladder -->

The line does what the strength rating could not. On these games the raw gap is **5.8 points**. The strength rating takes it to 2.4, the rating plus the jumper flags to 1.8, and the betting line to **1.1**. Add the jumper flags to the line and it is **0.9**. Those last two estimates have 95% ranges that include zero (−0.5 to +2.7, and −0.7 to +2.5). The result held when we let the link between price and winning bend, added season effects, removed the margin a different way, grouped the uncertainty by season, or kept the few unusual price pairs. Every variant landed between 1.09 and 1.11.

<!-- interactive:priced -->

Put it in everyday terms. In these games the teams that won the tip won 53.1% of the time. Before anyone touched the ball, the betting market had already rated those same teams at 52.6%. The market priced in about five-sixths of the edge, and what was left was half a point, give or take 0.8.

The line even knows about the tip. Bookmakers never price the jump ball, yet their price predicts which team wins it, and once you have the price, the strength rating adds nothing. That is the clearest sign that the tip is reading things the market already knew: lineups, health and who is likely to jump.

That is not proof the tip is worth nothing. Three cautions:

- **Indistinguishable from zero is not zero.** The 95% range runs to about 2.7 points, and a test of this size would reliably catch an effect only if it were larger than about 2. A small real effect is possible.
- **The line did clearly more than the strength rating.** On the same games, swapping the rating for the line lowers the estimate by about 1.3 points, and that drop is solid in its own right. The two estimates come from the same games, so their overlapping ranges understate it.
- **The prices have limits.** The source labels them closing lines but puts no timestamp on each quote, so we call them pregame lines. The archive stops after 2021-22. A broader archive of 15,466 games from 2007 to 2020 gives +0.92 for the line plus jumper flags, but it comes from the same source, so it is corroboration and not an independent replication.

None of this is a betting system. On these games, knowing the tip winner before the line closed would have been worth about half a point of win probability, give or take 0.8, against an average bookmaker margin of 3.8%. And nobody knows the tip winner before the tip.

## The IOU, and the one jump ball worth winning

There is one place where the tip looks like it matters. In games that are close entering the fourth quarter (within three points, about a fifth of all games), the team that won the opening tip wins **54.1%** of the time. The story writes itself. The tip winner gets the first possession of the fourth quarter, exactly when games are decided, so the tip must be worth something.

It is not, and the chart shows why. By the end of the third quarter the tip winner has had half a possession *fewer* than its opponent. It has opened one quarter and its opponent has opened two. Half a possession is worth about half a point (opening a period is worth about +0.46 points). So a game that is tied after three quarters is not really even. The tip winner has played its opponent level while short half a possession, and it is about to be paid back.

<!-- interactive:iou -->

Over the full 48 minutes the books balance to within a hundredth of a possession, which is why none of this adds anything to the 52.8%. It does make good in-game trivia. If the score is tied heading into the fourth, the team that won the opening tip is secretly about half a point ahead. In the roughly 1,200 games that were dead even after three quarters it went on to win 51.5%, too few games to say more than that.

Overtime is different, because overtime starts with a *new* jump ball, and this time nobody gets paid back. The period just ends. The team that wins the overtime tip ends up with about half a possession more than its opponent (**0.48** on average) in what starts as a tied game, and wins **55.7%** of the time (56.9% when there is only one overtime). Half a point is a lot in a period usually decided by four or five. By itself it is worth three or four points of win probability, so between half and two-thirds of that edge is real, mechanical value. A sliver of the rest is the familiar illusion, since the favorite wins the overtime tip 53% of the time, and the remainder is within the margin of error for 2,176 games.

| | Opening tip | Overtime tip |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Games | 36,765 | 2,176 |
| Won by the tip winner | 52.8% | 55.7% |
| Possessions gained | about 0, refunded by rule | +0.48, never refunded |

This is a different jump ball. The team that won the opening tip wins the overtime tip only 54% of the time, and only 6% of games get that far, so overtime explains essentially none of the opening-tip mystery (a few hundredths of a percentage point). It is the one jump ball all night that is worth winning.

## Where the five points go

Here is the whole accounting, built by adding one control at a time. The order matters, and these bars are not shares of causes. They show how much of the gap each added control removes.

<!-- interactive:ledger -->

- **Who you are.** Good teams have good centers, and good centers win tips. Team strength removes about 3 of the 5 points.
- **Who is on the floor.** The tip is a roll call for your starting center. Counting who took the tip removes about half a point more.
- **What the market knew.** On games with betting lines, the pregame price removes most of what the strength rating left behind.
- **What is left.** About a point, with a range that includes zero. The possession itself adds nothing, because the rules refund it.

Home court is held fixed in this accounting, because the comparison is home teams that won the tip against home teams that lost it. In the 52.8% headline, home court is worth about a quarter of a point.

> **The jump ball doesn't decide the game. It reads it.**

The opening tip is a thermometer, not a weapon. It reads out your roster, your center's health and your home crowd in one two-second gesture, and then, as far as the possession math can tell, contributes nothing we can detect to the next 48 minutes. Don't read "tip winners win 52.8%" as "the tip is worth 2.8 points of win probability." Read it as this: *teams that win tips were mostly going to win anyway, and the tip was kind enough to tell us early.*

The one exception comes later, if the game is still tied after 48 minutes. Nobody refunds that jump ball.

## What this shows, and what it does not

What it shows:

- The tip nets no possessions over a game, and the gap in win rate shrinks from five points to about one as the controls get stricter.
- Winning tips is a persistent individual skill, and the usual jumper being absent is a real signal.
- The overtime tip is worth roughly half a possession.

What it does not:

- It is not an experiment. Every adjusted number is an association, and a small causal effect of one or two points is still possible.
- The betting lines end in 2021-22 and carry no per-quote timestamps, so they may not be the true closing price.
- "Usual jumper" is defined after the fact, as whoever jumped most for a team that season. Defining it from earlier games only, as a bettor would have had to, moves the final estimate from 0.9 to about 1.1.
- It is not a betting strategy. It says nothing about prices available after the tip.

*How it was measured. Possession-level data covers 36,765 regular-season and playoff games, 1997-98 through 2025-26 (about 7 million possessions). Game winners come from official NBA results. Quarter-by-quarter points come from play-by-play, which differs from the official score, almost always by two points or fewer, in about 15% of games. "Winning the tip" means gaining the game's first possession, which matches the officially recorded jump-ball result in 99.98% of games. Jumpers are the actual jump-ball participants recorded in the league's play-by-play, available for 99.9% of games. "Team strength" is a rating updated after every game and built only from earlier results. Adjusted comparisons use regular-season games since 1999-2000 and carry a margin of about ±1 point. "Regular season" includes play-in games. Quarter-opening rules are in [NBA Rule 6](https://official.nba.com/rule-no-6-putting-ball-in-play-live-dead-ball/).*

*The betting-line test uses 12,937 regular-season games from 2011-12 to 2021-22, each matched to the official date, both teams and both final scores. A few games with implausible prices were set aside. The bookmaker's margin is removed by scaling the two implied probabilities to sum to one. Effects are average changes in home win probability from a logistic model, with 95% ranges robust to unequal variance. A second archive covering 11,221 of the same games had identical prices in 11,205.*
